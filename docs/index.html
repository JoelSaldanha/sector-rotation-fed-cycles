Sector Rotation and the Fed

How US equity sector returns vary across Federal Reserve rate cycles, 2000–2026

Joel Saldanha · Hugo Whyte · Parthiv Chakadath

May 2026 · Last updated 26 May 2026

Abstract

We analyse US equity sector returns from January 2000 to March 2026 grouped by Federal Reserve rate regime (hiking, cutting, neutral). Across the ten SPDR sector ETFs, sectors split cleanly along cyclicality: Technology, Utilities, Industrials, Consumer Discretionary and Financials lead during hiking cycles; Real Estate, Energy and Health Care lead during cutting cycles. The cyclical-defensive flip is consistent with the textbook framing of sector rotation through the business cycle, with one large caveat.

The cutting cycles in our sample (2001, 2008–09, 2020) all coincide with recessions, so cutting-cycle returns reflect crisis dynamics rather than normal monetary easing. We treat this as a conditional-mean comparison, not a causal claim.

The question

How do US equity sector returns vary across Federal Reserve rate hiking and cutting cycles, and which sectors consistently rotate into outperformance as monetary policy shifts?

We use the ten SPDR sector ETFs (XLK, XLF, XLE, XLV, XLU, XLY, XLP, XLI, XLB, XLRE) and classify every month from January 2000 to March 2026 into a rate regime based on the direction of the Federal Funds Rate. Then we compare annualised sector returns across regimes.

The split runs cleanly along sector cyclicality. Technology, Utilities, Industrials, Consumer Discretionary and Financials all post higher annualised returns under hiking than cutting. Technology and Financials show the widest gap (around 11 percentage points each), and Financials is the only sector with a negative cutting-cycle return at −1.6%.

Health Care and Real Estate flip the other way, with cutting-cycle returns above their hiking-cycle returns. Consumer Staples is roughly flat across the two regimes.

Materials and Energy sit between the two camps with broadly similar returns in both regimes. Energy in particular has a high cutting-cycle figure at 12.2%, which lines up with energy prices spiking during some of the crisis-driven easing periods in the sample.

The neutral regime is left out of this chart deliberately. With only 41 months across 10 sectors clustered in calm late-cycle bull runs, including neutral would flatter every sector indiscriminately and dilute the contrast that matters. See Finding 2 for the full picture and Limitations for the reasoning.

Finding 2

The hiking and cutting columns are roughly mirror images. Sectors that lead in hiking tend to lag in cutting and vice versa. Real Estate is the clearest case, at 2.2% in hiking and 14.9% in cutting (the highest cutting cell in the table). Financials posts the widest mirror-image swing of any sector, +9.3% hiking versus −1.6% cutting.

The neutral column is the highest for nine of the ten sectors. That isn't really telling us anything about how sectors behave in neutral periods. The 41 months in the neutral bucket happen to cluster in calm late-cycle bull runs that lifted almost everything, so the column ends up flattering every sector indiscriminately.

The Real Estate cutting figure (14.9%) reflects the 2020 COVID episode alone, since XLRE did not exist during the 2001 or 2008–09 cutting cycles. Treat it as one event rather than a pattern. See Limitations for the full XLRE caveat.

Finding 3

Every cutting cycle in this sample is a recession

The chart above shows what each regime produced. This one shows when each regime actually happened, which matters because the three cutting cycles in the sample all coincide with US recessions.

The red blocks at 2001–2003 (dot-com bust), 2007–2009 (GFC) and 2020 (COVID) are the only cutting episodes in the data. The Fed didn't cut rates in this sample without a crisis driving the decision.

That means the cutting-cycle returns in the bar chart and heatmap are crisis-conditioned rather than regime-conditioned in any clean theoretical sense. Any reading of how sectors perform when the Fed is cutting should really be read as how sectors perform when the Fed is cutting in a recession. A sample including a soft-landing cut would likely look different.

The shading is also noisy during flat-rate periods (most visibly 2010–2015 and 2021–2022). Where the Fed held rates flat, small month-to-month wobbles flipped the rolling-mean signal back and forth between hiking and cutting. The aggregated returns are robust to this because each regime cell averages over 100+ months, but the noise is worth showing rather than hiding. Tracked in repo Issue #9.

Method

We pulled monthly Federal Funds Rate, CPI, and the 10Y−2Y yield spread from FRED, and monthly prices for the ten SPDR sector ETFs (XLK, XLF, XLE, XLV, XLU, XLY, XLP, XLI, XLB, XLRE) from Alpha Vantage. Coverage runs from January 2000 to March 2026.

Rate regimes were labelled by taking a 3-month rolling mean of the Fed Funds Rate's monthly first difference. Positive means hiking, negative means cutting, zero or undefined means neutral. This is the standard approach for smoothing the noise in monthly rate-change data, though it produces some labelling flicker during flat-rate periods (see Limitations).

Returns are computed from month-end ETF prices as monthly returns, then aggregated to an annualised geometric figure within each (sector, regime) cell. The data quality check verified 2,959 sector-months across the ten ETFs with zero null prices, and confirmed the XLRE coverage gap of 124 observations versus 315 for every other sector.

The full pipeline is three notebooks in the repository. NB01_collect pulls the raw data from FRED and Alpha Vantage into JSON files. NB02_transform cleans, joins, and writes to a SQLite database. NB03_analyse reads from the database and produces the aggregations and figures shown on this page.

Limitations

Four caveats apply to the findings above. Each is acknowledged here in its consolidated form and discussed in context where it bites hardest.

1

Cutting cycles are all crisis-driven

The three cutting cycles in the sample (2001, 2008–09, 2020) all coincide with US recessions. Cutting-cycle returns reflect crisis dynamics rather than normal monetary easing. A sample including a soft-landing cut would likely produce different cutting-cycle figures.

2

Real Estate has a short history

XLRE launched in October 2015 and has 124 observations versus 315 for every other sector. Its cutting-cycle figure of 14.9% reflects the 2020 COVID episode alone, not three cutting cycles. Treat it as one event rather than a pattern.

3

The neutral sample is small and biased

Only 41 months across 10 sectors fall into the neutral bucket, and those months cluster in calm late-cycle bull runs. The neutral column flatters every sector and is excluded from the headline bar chart for this reason. The heatmap displays it with the colour scale capped so the visual signal is "this column is contextual, not comparable."

4

The rate-cycle labelling is noisy in flat-rate periods

During periods where the Fed held rates flat (most visibly 2010–2015 and 2021–2022), small month-to-month wobbles flipped the rolling-mean signal back and forth between hiking and cutting. This affects the shading on the Fed Funds chart only. The aggregated returns in Findings 1 and 2 are robust because each cell averages over 100+ months. Tracked in repo Issue #9.

What this analysis does not claim. This is a conditional-mean comparison, not a causal claim. Fed policy and sector returns are both driven by the underlying economic regime, and the analysis cannot separate "the Fed cut rates and therefore these sectors did well" from "a recession was happening, the Fed cut rates because of it, and these sectors did well for separate reasons." Treat the findings as descriptive of historical co-occurrence, not predictive of future cycles.

Conclusion

Across the 2000–2026 sample, US equity sectors split cleanly along cyclicality when grouped by Fed rate regime, but the cleanest reading of the data is that cutting-cycle outperformance for defensives and REITs is a recession story rather than a monetary easing story. The cyclical-defensive flip is real; the causal mechanism is not what the labels suggest.

For an investor or analyst working with this framework, the practical implication is that sector rotation strategies that condition on Fed regime are implicitly conditioning on the macro environment that produced that regime. A strategy built on "buy defensives when the Fed cuts" is, in this sample, the same strategy as "buy defensives during recessions." That is not necessarily wrong, but it is worth being precise about what the model is doing.

Reproducing the analysis

The repository is structured so a stranger can clone and re-run from scratch. The pipeline needs API keys for FRED (free) and Alpha Vantage (free tier sufficient).

  1. Install dependencies from requirements.txt: 
    pip install -r requirements.txt
  2. Create a .env file in the root directory containing your API keys: 
    FRED_API_KEY=your_fred_key_here
ALPHA_VANTAGE_KEY=your_alpha_vantage_key_here
    FRED keys are obtained at fredaccount.stlouisfed.org/apikey. Alpha Vantage keys at alphavantage.co/support/#api-key.
  3. Install dependencies. 
    pip install pandas numpy matplotlib seaborn requests python-dotenv jupyter
  4. Run the notebooks in order: NB01_collect → NB02_transform → NB03_analyse. Outputs land in data/processed/analysis/ and docs/figures/. NB03 Section 10 exports the JS constants used by this website's interactive charts.

The analysis was built against a snapshot of FRED and Alpha Vantage data as of late May 2026. Re-fetching may produce slightly different figures for the latest months, but the historical findings (2000–2024) are stable.
